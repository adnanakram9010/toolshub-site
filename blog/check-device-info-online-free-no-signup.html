How to Check Device Info Online

Your screen, browser, CPU and memory — read live from your browser in seconds, free and private.

November 19, 2026 · 5 min read

You can check your device info online in seconds: open ToolsHub's Device Info tool and it reads 12 live details straight from your browser — screen resolution, viewport size, browser name and version, CPU cores, memory, timezone, language, touch support and more. No signup, no download, nothing sent anywhere: every value is read locally.

Most people need these details only occasionally, but when you need them, you need them exactly: a support agent asks for your browser version, a designer for your screen resolution, and a broken website leaves you wondering whether the fault is your screen, your browser, or the site. Digging through settings for each answer takes minutes — the tool puts all twelve on one screen.

What the tool shows you

The grid fills in automatically. Here is what each detail means:

  • Screen resolution — the full size of your display in pixels, for example 1920 × 1080.
  • Viewport size — the area your browser uses for web pages, for example 1920 × 937 — smaller than the screen, because tabs and the taskbar take up space.
  • Device pixel ratio — physical pixels per CSS pixel — 1 is one-to-one, 2 means two physical pixels per CSS pixel.
  • Platform — your operating system as your browser reports it, such as Win32 or MacIntel.
  • Browser — the browser name and major version, for example Google Chrome 131 or Microsoft Edge 131.
  • CPU cores — how many processor cores your browser can use — typically 4, 8, or 16.
  • Device memory — approximate RAM in GB — Chrome only; Firefox and Safari show "Not available".
  • Online status — Online or Offline, updating live if your connection drops or returns.
  • Timezone — the timezone your device is set to, for example Asia/Karachi.
  • Language — your browser's language setting, for example en-GB.
  • Touch support — whether your screen supports touch, and how many touch points it tracks.
  • Cookies enabled — Yes or No — most sites need cookies on to keep you signed in.

Check your device info in 4 steps

  1. Open the Device Info tool. The grid fills in automatically — nothing to type, nothing to install.
  2. Read the 12 details. Scan the grid for the value you need — screen resolution for a designer, browser and version for a support ticket, timezone before a call.
  3. Resize or rotate, then press Refresh. Make the window smaller, rotate your phone, or switch monitors — then press Refresh and the tool re-reads every live value, the quickest way to see how your viewport changes.

Worked example 1: screen resolution vs viewport size

Take a laptop with a 1920 × 1080 screen. The tool shows two numbers: screen resolution 1920 × 1080, and a viewport of, say, 1920 × 937 — 143 pixels shorter (1080 − 937 = 143), taken by the tab bar, address bar and bookmarks bar, plus the Windows taskbar.

A page is laid out for the viewport, not the screen, so designing for 1080 pixels of height puts the bottom 143 below the fold. Always check the viewport to know how much of a page is actually visible.

Worked example 2: device pixel ratio and real pixels

Device pixel ratio (DPR) is how many physical pixels hide behind one CSS pixel. A phone with a 390-pixel viewport at DPR 3 shows 390 × 3 = 1,170 physical pixels across. A laptop with a 1440-pixel viewport at DPR 2 shows 1440 × 2 = 2,880. A monitor at DPR 1.25 with a 1920-pixel viewport shows 1920 × 1.25 = 2,400.

That is why a 300-pixel-wide image looks blurry on a phone but sharp on a monitor: at DPR 3 it stretches across 300 × 3 = 900 physical pixels, at DPR 1 it fills exactly 300. If an image looks soft on your phone, check the DPR first — the screen is fine, the image is too small for the density.

Worked example 3: how the tool names your browser

The tool reads your browser from the user-agent string, checking in a careful order. Edge's string holds both "Edg/131.0.0.0" and "Chrome/131.0.0.0", because Edge is built on the Chromium engine — the tool checks Edge first, so it correctly reports "Microsoft Edge 131" rather than Chrome. A Chrome string gives "Google Chrome 131", and a Safari one with "Version/18.2" gives "Apple Safari 18".

The version is the useful part: two people both "on Chrome" can see different behaviour on 131 versus 118 — give the name and the version together.

5 mistakes to avoid when checking device info

  • Confusing screen resolution with viewport size. The screen is your display, the viewport is the space left for web pages — quoting 1920 × 1080 when someone asks for your viewport misleads them by 143 pixels or more.
  • Assuming a pixel ratio of 1 means a bad screen. Most desktop monitors are DPR 1 and perfectly sharp. A high DPR matters on small, dense phone screens; on a 24-inch monitor, 1 is normal.
  • Treating device memory as an exact figure. Chrome rounds it to values such as 4 or 8 GB and labels it approximate. "Not available" in Firefox or Safari is normal — those browsers do not share the figure.
  • Trusting the browser name without the version. Edge and Chrome share the same engine, so the name alone does not identify your browser. Include the version in a bug report — "Edge 131" and "Chrome 131" are different browsers that share an engine.
  • Sharing your full device details with untrusted sites. Screen size, browser version, timezone and language can fingerprint a browser. ToolsHub reads them locally and sends nothing anywhere — only share the full list with a support team you trust.

Frequently asked questions

What is device info?
The details your browser knows about your device: screen resolution, viewport size, browser name and version, CPU cores, approximate memory, timezone, language, touch support and cookies. ToolsHub's Device Info tool reads all 12 live.

Is it safe to check device info online?
Yes. Everything is read locally in your browser and nothing is uploaded, so the details never leave your device. Do not paste them into untrusted websites, though — the same details can fingerprint a browser.

Why is my viewport smaller than my screen resolution?
Screen resolution is the full size of your display, for example 1920 × 1080 pixels. The viewport is the area your browser actually uses for web pages — tabs, the address bar and the taskbar take up space, so it might be 1920 × 937, 143 pixels shorter.

Why does device memory say "Not available"?
Only Chrome-based browsers share an approximate memory figure, rounded to values such as 4 or 8 GB. Firefox and Safari do not expose device memory at all, so the tool honestly shows "Not available" instead of guessing.
