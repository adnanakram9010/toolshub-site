How to Use a Pomodoro Timer Online

Work in focused 25-minute bursts, rest properly between them, and let the timer handle the switching — free, no signup.

October 28, 2026 · 6 min read

Most unfinished work is not hard work — it is work that never got an uninterrupted stretch of attention. You sit down to write a report, check one message, and suddenly forty minutes have gone and the report is still on its first paragraph. The Pomodoro Technique fixes this with a deliberately small promise: focus on one thing for 25 minutes, then stop, rest, and repeat.

The technique is named after the tomato-shaped kitchen timer its inventor used in the 1980s, and it has survived for a simple reason — a short, visible countdown makes starting easy and quitting hard. You do not need willpower for a whole afternoon, only for the next 25 minutes. This guide shows you how to use a Pomodoro timer online, what each control does, and how to build the habit so it actually sticks.

What a Pomodoro timer does

A Pomodoro timer alternates between focus sessions and breaks, and switches between them automatically. One "Pomodoro" is a single focus session — 25 minutes by default. After each focus session comes a short break of 5 minutes. After every four focus sessions comes a longer break of 15 minutes, which is when you properly step away: refill your water, stretch, look out of the window.

The Pomodoro Timer shows the current phase ("FOCUS" or break), the time remaining, four session-progress circles, and a count of completed sessions. It runs entirely in your browser, so no timer, setting, or data is sent anywhere, and there is no account to create.

Use the Pomodoro timer in 5 steps

  1. Open the Pomodoro Timer — it starts ready to go: "FOCUS" and 25:00 on the display, with zero sessions completed.
  2. Set your lengths if you want. The focus length (25 minutes), short break (5 minutes) and long break (15 minutes) are editable number fields, so you can adjust them before you start — for example, 50-minute focus sessions with 10-minute breaks for deep writing. The classic 25 / 5 / 15 split is the best starting point if you have never tried the technique.
  3. Pick one task and press Start. Write the task down somewhere visible, then work on that task and nothing else until the timer ends.
  4. Take the break when the beep sounds. The timer switches to a break automatically and plays a gentle beep, so you do not have to watch the clock. Step away from the screen during breaks: the break only restores your attention if you actually rest.
  5. Repeat, and let the long break reset you. After every four focus sessions the timer gives you the 15-minute long break instead of a short one — then start the next set of four.

The other controls are simple: Pause stops the countdown if you are interrupted; Skip jumps to the next phase without counting a session, which is useful when a session is genuinely over early; and Reset returns the timer to the start of a focus session.

A worked example: one morning of focused work

Say you plan a morning of four Pomodoros before lunch, using the default 25 / 5 / 15 settings. The maths of the morning looks like this:

  • Focus time: 4 sessions × 25 minutes = 100 minutes of real, single-task work.
  • Breaks: 3 short breaks × 5 minutes + 1 long break × 15 minutes = 30 minutes of rest.
  • Total elapsed: 100 + 30 = 130 minutes, or 2 hours 10 minutes from the first Start to the end of the long break.

Stretch the same pattern across a full workday of eight Pomodoros and the numbers scale cleanly: 8 × 25 = 200 minutes of focus (3 hours 20 minutes), plus breaks of 60 minutes in total (six 5-minute short breaks and two 15-minute long breaks), for 260 minutes — 4 hours 20 minutes — from start to finish. Do four Pomodoros a day, five days a week, and you have 20 sessions: 500 minutes, or 8 hours 20 minutes, of genuinely focused work in a week.

Pomodoro timer vs stopwatch vs countdown timer

All three count time, but they serve different jobs:

  • Pomodoro timer: best for sustained work — it enforces the focus/break rhythm for you, switching phases and beeping automatically, so you never have to decide when to stop.
  • Stopwatch: best for measuring how long something actually took — useful afterwards, when you want to know whether a task took 20 minutes or 50.
  • Countdown timer: best for a single deadline — one countdown to zero, such as "finish this draft in 40 minutes", with no built-in break cycle.

If your problem is drifting attention rather than a single deadline, the Pomodoro timer is the right tool of the three. If you need to time a presentation or a workout instead, the Stopwatch or Countdown Timer will serve you better.

5 mistakes that break the Pomodoro Technique

  • Skipping the breaks. The break is part of the technique, not a reward for finishing early. Working through a break feels productive and leaves you drained by the third session — take the full 5 minutes, away from the screen.
  • Checking messages "just for a second" mid-session. One glance breaks the single-task rule and usually costs far more than a second. Write distractions down and deal with them in the break.
  • Starting with sessions that are too long. Fifty-minute focus sessions sound efficient, but if you cannot sustain them you will quit the technique, not the session. Start at 25 minutes; lengthen only after a week of consistent sessions.
  • Counting interrupted sessions as complete. If a session is derailed by a real interruption, it does not count — use Skip to move on and start a fresh one. Padding the count teaches you that the count means nothing.
  • Using the timer without choosing a task first. A countdown with no task attached becomes background noise. Every session starts with one named task, written down where you can see it.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Pomodoro Technique?
The Pomodoro Technique is a time-management method built on 25-minute focus sessions separated by short breaks, with a longer break after every four sessions. The fixed, short sessions make it easy to start and hard to drift, and the breaks protect your attention across a full day.

What are the default Pomodoro timer settings?
Focus length 25 minutes, short break 5 minutes, and long break 15 minutes after every four focus sessions. All three are editable in the Pomodoro Timer before you press Start.

How many Pomodoros should I do in a day?
Start with four — one morning set, which is 100 minutes of focus in 2 hours 10 minutes including breaks. Build up gradually; eight Pomodoros (200 minutes of focus) is a strong full workday. Consistency over a week beats a single heroic day.

Do I need an account or app to use it?
No. The timer runs entirely in your browser — no signup, no download, and nothing you enter is sent anywhere. Open the page, press Start, and work.
